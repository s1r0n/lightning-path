## The Origin of the Credit Card

Did you know the term “credit card” was coined by [Edward Bellamy](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Edward_Bellamy)? Edward was a journalist and cousin to Christian socialist Francis Bellamy, the man who wrote the Pledge of Allegiance. The term appears in Bellamy’s 1888 utopian novel [Looking Backward](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/lindex.php/Looking_Backward), 2000–1887. Curiously, this wasn’t the credit card as instantiated by The Family. This was a device for recording entitlement, not debt. A container of guaranteed income provided by the wealth generated by a nationalized and the abolishment of money. In the place of money, the card provided for the individual ” credit corresponding to [their] share of the annual product of the nation,” a portion “given to every citizen on the public books at the beginning of each year, and a credit card issued him with which [they] procure at the public storehouses, found in every community, whatever [they desire] whenever they [desire it]” (ch. 9).

The mechanics are worth a look, because functionally Bellamy’s card is a debit card drawn on a guaranteed income account. Every citizen, man or woman, worker or invalid, starts the year with an equal credit, recharged annually. A clerk pricks out squares on the pasteboard as goods are drawn. There is no borrowing, no interest, no minimum payment, no possibility of debt. Even the “dollars” printed on the card have been hollowed out: “We have kept the old word, but not the substance,” Dr. Leete explains — they survive only as “an algebraical symbol for comparing the values of products with one another.” The card’s whole economic function is to record what every member of society is guaranteed — “the nurture, education, and comfortable maintenance of every citizen from the cradle to the grave.”

Note that was not a politically neutral payment tech. Bellamy’s card was rooted in Christian socialism and utopian thinking — in the conviction, which his cousin Francis preached until it cost him his Boston pulpit, that solidarity is a religious obligation and that “the golden age lies before us and is not far behind us.” Bellamy’s own Nationalist movement framed the equalization of material life as the practical realization of Christian brotherhood, and the novel itself closes not with an economics lecture but with a Sunday sermon. Which gives us the perfect inversion to ponder: Bellamy’s credit card recorded what society owes you. Ours records what you owe The Family. Same word. Opposite regimes. One a Regime of Accumulation controlled by The Family. The other a Regime of Distribution, organized for the benefit of all.
