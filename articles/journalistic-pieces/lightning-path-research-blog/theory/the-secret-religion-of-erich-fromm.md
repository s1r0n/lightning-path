# The Religion That Shall Not Be Named

Imagine a typical weekday morning. A commuter rushes to catch a train, checks stock prices on a smartphone, and mentally rehearses a presentation for a quarterly review. No prayers are uttered, no incense is burned, and no scriptures are consulted. And yet, Erich Fromm would insist that this person is deeply, devoutly religious.

In his provocative psychoanalytic and sociological works, Fromm argues that modern industrial society harbours a <u>secret religion</u>, a system of devotion that operates entirely beneath the radar of conscious awareness (Fromm, 1955, 1976). He calls it the Industrial Religion. It is the real faith of the post-Reformation West, a framework of orientation and an object of worship centered on work, property, profit, and power. Its primary effect, according to Fromm, is to transform human beings from ends in themselves into mere servants of the economic machinery they themselves have constructed.

For the student encountering Fromm for the first time, his thesis is bracing. It suggests that the secularization narrative, the idea that modernity gradually shed its religious skin, is a comforting illusion. We did not stop worshipping; we merely changed the object of our worship. Crucially, this new cult hides behind a Christian façade, using its vocabulary while structurally undermining its core ethics of love, mercy, and solidarity. The result is a profound alienation, where the *homo faber* (man the maker) becomes enslaved to his own creations.

In this article, we will unpack Fromm's core argument regarding the patriarchal and matriarchal elements of this transition, examine why his framework remains philosophically provocative for understanding modern life, and then turn a critical eye toward his theological and historical scholarship to ask: Is this analysis fair?

## The Rise of the Industrial Religion: From Motherly Love to Fatherly Authority

Fromm's critique begins not with economics, but with the history of religious psychology (Fromm, 1941). He distinguishes between two fundamental principles that have shaped Western spirituality:

- **The Motherly Principle**: Rooted in unconditional love, mercy, and natural equality. It says, "I love you because you are my child," regardless of achievement.
- **The Fatherly Principle**: Rooted in conditional love, authority, law, and justice. It says, "I love you because you obey and achieve."

For Fromm, the medieval Catholic Church maintained a fragile but vital synthesis of these two poles. The Virgin Mary and the priestly class embodied motherly mercy alongside the strict justice of God the Father. This synthesis, he argued, corresponded to a more cooperative relationship with nature.

That synthesis shattered with Martin Luther and the Protestant Reformation in Northern Europe. Fromm contends that Luther eliminated the "motherly element" entirely, establishing a purely patriarchal form of Christianity (Fromm, 1941). This new religious psychology was perfectly calibrated for the emerging urban middle class and secular princes. Its social character was built on:

- Submission to a powerful, punitive male authority.
- Guilt as the primary motivator (driven by the fear of disobedience).
- Work as the sole path to divine approval and worldly success.
- Atomization, dissolving bonds of human solidarity in favour of self-interest and mutual antagonism.

Behind this austere Christian façade, the Industrial Religion crept into existence. While people continued to speak of "Christian values," their actual daily practice—their ultimate concern—bent toward class capitulation and capital accumulation (Fromm, 1955). The sacred objects became not relics or altars, but piece rates, production quotas, property titles, profit margins, and hierarchical power.

## A Philosophical Provocation: Rethinking Religion Itself

Was Fromm accurate in his depictions? Before we scrutinize Fromm's accuracy, we must pause to appreciate the sheer philosophical force of his argument. For a student, his terminology is a powerful intellectual tool that forces us to rethink the very definition of religion.

First, Fromm shifts religion from creed to practice. He follows in the footsteps of the existentialist philosopher Paul Tillich, who defined religion as one's "ultimate concern" (Tillich, 1957). By this metric, a financier obsessed with quarterly earnings is just as "religious," just as "devoted," as a monk in silent prayer. This is a radical hermeneutic that allows us to read economic behaviour as spiritual behaviour. It makes visible the implicit liturgical structure of capitalism, the daily rituals (commuting, clicking, producing), the sacred texts (spreadsheets, market reports), and the high priests (CEOs, political leaders, brothers at the lodge).

Second, Fromm provides a dialectical understanding of freedom. The Industrial Religion praises "individualism" and "freedom," yet Fromm identifies this as a constrained, narrow freedom, the freedom to choose between preordained career paths, not the freedom to unplug from the system or to reimagine one's own purpose (Fromm, 1941).

Third, his synthesis of Marx and Freud is intellectually seductive. Fromm marries Marx's critique of alienation (we are estranged from our labour) with Freud's critique of repression (we are estranged from our desires). The Industrial Religion, Fromm suggests, is a mass neurosis. It channels human energy into capitalist productivity and as a consequence becomes an existential neurosis and spiritual pathology (Fromm, 1955).

## A Critical Examination

Having acknowledged the provocative power of Fromm's framework, we must now turn to the uncomfortable question of fairness. Is Fromm's assessment of religious history, particularly his attribution of modern ills to the "patriarchal" turn in Protestantism, historically and theologically sound? The answer is no. While Fromm is a brilliant diagnostician of the present, he is a reckless historian of the past.

### 1. The Theological Misreading of Grace and Election

Fromm's most significant error lies in his interpretation of Lutheran and Calvinist theology. He portrays the patriarchal God as a stern taskmaster who demands obedience and offers conditional love based on "works" (e.g., productive labour). However, this is nearly the exact opposite of Reformation theology.

The central tenet of Luther and Calvin is *sola gratia*—salvation by unconditional grace alone, entirely independent of human works. When Fromm argues that the Protestant social character seeks to "earn" love and approval through labour, he ironically describes a Catholic heresy, not the Reformation (Fromm, 1941). The "election" that Calvin speaks of is not a reward for patriarchal favouritism; it is a terrifyingly arbitrary gift that cannot be purchased by any amount of toil.

### 2. Ignoring the Persistent Maternal Imagery

Fromm claims Luther excised the "motherly element." But even a cursory glance at Reformation history complicates this. Lutheran hymns frequently use maternal imagery to describe God's compassion. More broadly, in Catholic, Orthodox, and even Anglican traditions, the figure of Mary, the Virgin, remains an overwhelmingly powerful symbol of maternal intercession. The Church is frequently described as Mother Church. Fromm's binary framework forces him to ignore these enduring traditions to maintain his narrative of a purely cold, patriarchal modernity.

### 3. Fragile Anthropological Foundations

Fromm's theory of a prehistoric "matriarchal" society overthrown by a "patriarchal" one rests almost entirely on the work of J.J. Bachofen, a 19th-century Swiss jurist whose scholarship is now widely regarded as speculative and romanticized (Bachofen, 1861/1967). Bachofen projected 19th-century gender norms onto prehistory. Fromm himself reluctantly admitted that "many objections" to Bachofen were legitimate, yet he built his psychological architecture upon this unstable foundation. This is a classic case of building a skyscraper on swampy ground.

### 4. Gender Essentialism and the Blind Spot of Feminism

Paradoxically, Fromm's critique of patriarchy is itself deeply patriarchal in its assumptions. He essentializes gender, biologically tying women to "nature," "the womb," and "unconditional love," while men are tied to "culture," "law," and "conditional authority." This replicates the very binary that feminist philosophers have spent decades deconstructing. It suggests that maternal values are intrinsic to female biology rather than cultural choices, a position that is both scientifically and philosophically problematic. Moreover, by framing history as a linear fall from a golden "matriarchal" age into a fallen "patriarchal" one, Fromm indulges in a nostalgic primitivism that fails to account for the actual complexities of historical power dynamics.

## A Sociological Turn

Despite its flaws, Fromm's theory of religion brilliantly reveals the ideological architecture of Industrial Religion—its rituals, its sacred objects, its hidden liturgy (Fromm, 1955, 1976). However, he ultimately locates its genesis in a nebulous psychological transformation: the shift from matriarchal to patriarchal character structures, or the universal human "fear of freedom," a questionable concept at best (Fromm, 1941). For all his debt to Marx, Fromm remains, at this decisive juncture, more Freudian than Marxist.

This is precisely where we must refine and correct his critique. Fromm's mistake was in blaming the symptom (patriarchal religion) rather than the deeper, structural cause: the active interference by the Accumulating Class.

The Industrial Religion was not a spontaneous cultural psychosis, nor was it the inevitable psychological byproduct of Luther's theology. It was, rather, a strategic project, a hegemonic apparatus actively cultivated, financed, legislated, and enforced by the rising merchant capitalists, early industrialists, and their political allies. To be clear, the Protestant work ethic and the patriarchal social character did not merely coincide with the interests of primitive accumulation; they were weaponized by a social class that recognized in guilt-ridden obedience and atomized self-interest the ideal psychological conditions for wage labour, social discipline, and the endless expansion of capital.

Consider the historical evidence Fromm overlooks: the Poor Laws that criminalized idleness, the factory discipline that broke communal rhythms, the educational reforms that inculcated punctuality and subservience, and the theological sermons that explicitly equated poverty with moral failure and wealth with divine favour. These were class interventions. The Accumulating Class used religion to actively manufacture the doctrinal core of Capitalism, using the pulpit, the magistrate, and eventually the schoolroom to naturalize inequality and sanctify accumulation as God's will.

Religion, in this materialist reading, was never the true father of capitalism. Rather, the Accumulating Class seized upon existing religious narratives and re-forged them into a disciplinary instrument. Patriarchal authority, the cult of work, and the dissolution of solidarity were not psychic archetypes that "accidentally" shaped the economy; they were ideological tools deliberately sharpened to extract surplus value, pacify the dispossessed, and legitimize the new social order.

To fully overcome the Industrial Religion, we must move beyond Fromm's therapeutic humanism. A purely psychological awakening, learning to "love" rather than "have," is insufficient if the material conditions that reward accumulation and punish solidarity remain intact (Fromm, 1976). True emancipation demands a structural confrontation with the class that actively profits from our spiritual servitude. Only by locating the agency of our alienation in concrete material interests, rather than in abstract historical forces or universal psychic fears, can we begin to repossess not merely our individual souls, but our collective means of existence.

By wrestling with Fromm's brilliance and correcting his blindness, we arrive at a sharper truth: ideologies are not diseases that befall societies; they are instruments wielded by those who stand to gain. To critique the Industrial Religion is therefore not merely to critique a mindset, but to name the class that built it, sustains it, and defends it, and to organize accordingly.
