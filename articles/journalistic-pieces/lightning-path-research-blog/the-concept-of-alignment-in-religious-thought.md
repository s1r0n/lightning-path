# The Concept of Alignment in Religious Thought

In my article on [alignment in utopian writing](https://www.lightningpath.org/lightning-strike/the-concept-of-alignment-in-utopian-writing/), I ended with a teaser: the concept of [Alignment](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Alignment) shows up not only in the work of utopians and social critics, but in the religious corpus of every major tradition on this planet. In a companion piece, I surveyed the scholarly vocabularies — eudaimonia, individuation, the real self, congruence, autonomy, authenticity. This article completes the trilogy. Here, we go into the religious record.

It is worth noting at the outset that a scholar saw this clearly over a century ago. William James, the father of the psychology of religion, offered what is functionally a one-line definition of alignment: religion, he said, "consists of the belief that there is an unseen order, and that our supreme good lies in harmoniously adjusting ourselves thereto" (James, 1902/1982, p. 53). An unseen order, and the need to harmoniously adjust ourselves to it. That is alignment, stated in 1902, by a scientist, about religion.

## A Quick Theoretical Note

On the Lightning Path, [Alignment](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Alignment) is the extent to which the [Bodily Ego](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Bodily_Ego) — the little ego of the physical body, neurologically rooted in the brain's Default Mode Network — is in concordance with the [Spiritual Ego](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Spiritual_Ego), the higher self, the bit of [Consciousness](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Fabric_of_Consciousness) that animates the body. Alignment is one of the [Seven Essential Needs](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Seven_Essential_Needs), and it is a requirement of strong [Connection](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Connection).

The religious traditions say the same thing in their own vocabularies. The "unseen order" goes by many names — God, the Self, the Tao, Asha, the will of Allah — and the state of concordance with it goes by many names too: righteousness, holiness, dharma, wu wei, submission. Meanwhile, misalignment also has its religious names: sin, the Lie, unwholesome action, striving and contending. And most traditions embed what I would call an Alignment Rule Set — a practical system for establishing and maintaining concordance. Consider the following.

## Alignment in the Traditions

**Zoroastrianism — *Asha* and the Lie.** One of the oldest alignment frameworks on record. Zoroastrian cosmology is structured around the opposition between *Asha* — truth, order, righteousness, the way things are meant to be — and *Druj*, the Lie, disorder, disjuncture. The religious life is the practice of aligning thought, word, and deed with Asha. Zoroaster offered "six cardinal virtues of Ahura Mazda" which individuals were supposed to emulate: "Good Mind," Righteousness, "Divine Kingdom," "Devotion," "Perfection," and "Immortality" (Dhalla, 1938). Alignment as emulation of the divine order, in other words — and a moral vocabulary that names misalignment as a lie you live.

**Hinduism — the self and the Self, and *svadharma*.** The *Bhagavad Gita* contains what may be the single most explicit statement of alignment in any scripture, distinguishing the little self from the big Self and making their concordance the whole point:

> He should raise the self by the Self; he should not let the self sink; for, [as] the self is indeed the friend of the Self, [so also] is the self indeed Self's enemy. The self is the friend of the Self of him whose self is conquered by the Self; but for [him who is] bereft-of-Self, the self is like an enemy in enmity. (Bhagavad Gita 6.5–6.6; Feuerstein & Feuerstein, 2011, p. 157)

The aligned Bodily Ego is the Self's friend; the damaged, unaligned one is its enemy. The Gita adds a second alignment teaching in the doctrine of *svadharma* — one's own duty, one's own path: "Better is one's own duty, though devoid of merit, than the duty of another well discharged. Better is death in one's own duty; the duty of another is fraught with fear" (Bhagavad Gita 3.35; Sivananda, 1995). Live your own truth, however imperfectly; do not perform someone else's, however well.

**Buddhism — the wholesome and the unwholesome, and the Eightfold Path.** Speaking of the Buddha's earliest teachings, Bhikkhu Bodhi notes that "the initial response the Buddha intends to arouse in us is an ethical one. By calling our attention to our bondage to old age and death, he seeks to inspire in us a firm resolution to turn away from unwholesome ways of living and to embrace instead wholesome alternatives" (Bodhi, 2005, p. 17). Wholesome and unwholesome — aligned and disjunctive. And the Noble Eightfold Path itself — right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration — is nothing if not an Alignment Rule Set, a disciplined program of [Right Thought](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Right_Thought), [Right Action](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Right_Action), and right orientation, two and a half millennia old.

**Taoism — water and the Way.** The *Tao Te Ching* makes alignment with the Tao — the unseen order James pointed to — the whole art of living:

> The highest good is like water. Water gives life to the ten thousand things and does not strive. It flows in places men reject and so is like the Tao. (Lao Tzu, 1972, ch. 8)

The Taoist name for the practice is *wu wei* — effortless action, action without contending, action that moves *with* the grain of the cosmos rather than against it. The aligned person, like water, does not strive — and precisely for that reason, "no fight, no blame" (Lao Tzu, 1972, ch. 8).

**Christianity — "not my will, but thine."** The clearest Christian statement of alignment comes at the crisis point of the Gospels, in the Garden of Gethsemane: "Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done" (Luke 22:42, King James Version). The same formula sits at the centre of the prayer Jesus taught: "Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10). Note what this is: not passivity, but a deliberate subordination of the little will of the Bodily Ego to the will of the higher self — the vehicle becoming responsive to the driver. Augustine said the same thing existentially, in the most famous sentence of the *Confessions*: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you" (Augustine, 1991, 1.1.1). Restlessness — anxiety, despair, the feeling of being out of joint — is what misalignment feels like from the inside.

**Islam and Sufism — submission and the perfect reflection.** The word *Islam* itself means submission — concordance with the will of Allah, alignment as the name of the religion itself. Islamic spirituality formalizes the work as *tahdhīb al-akhlāq*, the refinement of character. In the Sufi tradition, alignment is complete concordance, expressed in lover's terms: "…when a human becomes a perfect slave of God, then God becomes the eye and ear of that person, who becomes all light and a perfect reflection of God's qualities" (Ernst, 1997, p. 155). The Bodily Ego as a perfect reflection of the Spiritual Ego — that is the definition, nearly word for word.

**Indigenous traditions — "the proper manner."** Finally, alignment is not only a scriptural idea. Barbara Myerhoff's account of the Huichol makes the point as clearly as any theology:

> The good life is the religious life. … An evil man is not truly Huichol, for to be Huichol is to live in the proper manner. … It seems to embrace above all the concept of attaining wholeness and harmony. To be in accord with one another, with oneself, with one's customs — this is the state of being a proper Huichol and it is sacred. (Myerhoff, 1974, pp. 73–74)

Accord with oneself, with one another, with the order of things — inner alignment and outer alignment together, held as the sacred centre of a whole way of life.

## The Takeaway

Asha and the Lie. The self as friend or enemy of the Self. Wholesome and unwholesome. Water and the Way. "Not my will, but thine." Submission. The proper manner. Every major tradition on this planet has a name for the need, a name for the failure, and a practical discipline for closing the gap. The vocabulary is religious; the observation is the same one the utopians made, and the same one the clinicians keep confirming.

Two cautions, briefly. First, the presence of alignment teachings in a tradition does not mean the institution was faithful to them — I have argued elsewhere that elites have repeatedly colonized these traditions and bent their archetypes toward control, so the authentic core has to be distinguished from the ideological wrapper (Sosteric, 2014). Second, the religious vocabularies, for all their power, are easy to mystify. What the Lightning Path offers is a demystified umbrella: [Alignment](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Alignment) as an essential need, attainable in practice through the [Three Rs of Alignment](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Three_Rs_of_Alignment) — Right Thought, Right Action, Right Environment — in service of [Connection](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/index.php/Connection), which is the goal toward which all of these traditions, at their best, were always pointing.
