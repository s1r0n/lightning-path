# The Larger Sutra on the Land of Bliss

Written down in Sanskrit around the first or second century CE, the Larger Sutra on the Land of Bliss is the founding scripture of **Pure Land Buddhism**,  today the most widely practiced form of Buddhism in East Asia. It tells the story of a monk named Dharmakara who, countless ages ago, vowed to create a perfect world where anyone could be reborn simply by calling his name. This sutra describes Sukhavati (Heaven), the Land of Bliss in extravagant detail. At its heart is a revolutionary promise: liberation is not reserved for saints and sages. Ordinary people, even at the very end of life, can reach a realm where suffering is unknown and enlightenment is guaranteed. 

This rendering condenses the text to its essentials, in everyday language.

## 1. The Buddha Glows

The Buddha was staying on Vulture Peak near Rajagriha, with thirty-two thousand monks and a great company of bodhisattvas led by Maitreya. His disciple Ananda noticed that the Buddha's face was shining with unusual brightness — clear and golden, like refined gold laid on pale cloth. "You must be contemplating the Buddhas of past, present, and future," Ananda said. "What are you seeing?"

The Buddha praised the question. "You have asked this for the benefit of the whole world. Listen well."

## 2. The Monk Dharmakara

Long ago, immeasurably long, a line of eighty-one Buddhas appeared in the world, one after another, ending with a Buddha called Lokesvararaja. In his time lived a monk named Dharmakara, a man of deep memory, wisdom, and noble character. Dharmakara came before that Buddha, praised him in verse, and said: "I want to attain supreme enlightenment. Teach me what a Buddha and a Buddha's land should be, so that I can create one."

Lokesvararaja taught him, for millions of years, the perfections of countless billions of Buddha-lands, describing all their excellent qualities. Then Dharmakara withdrew and meditated for five cosmic ages, distilling the finest features of all those lands into a single, perfect land of his own. He returned and announced: "I have formed my vows."

## 3. The Vows

Standing before the Buddha, Dharmakara declared what his land would be, each vow ending: *"If this is not so, may I never attain supreme enlightenment."*

**About the land itself:** There will be no hells, no animal rebirth, no hungry ghosts, no warring demigods. Everyone born there will be golden in color, with no distinction between gods and humans except as a figure of speech. No one will have any sense of possession — not even of their own body. The very word "sin" will not exist there. Its beauty will be beyond measure . No one, even with divine sight, could ever finish describing it. Its radiance will mirror all the worlds of the universe, the way a face appears in a polished mirror. Fragrant incense will rise from countless jeweled vases, and showers of jewel-flowers and clouds of music will fall without ceasing.

**About the beings born there:** They will be able to travel across billions of worlds in a single thought. They will remember their past lives, see with the divine eye, hear the teachings of distant Buddhas with the divine ear, and know the minds of others. They will be established in truth, guaranteed to reach final nirvana,  bound to only one more birth before full enlightenment. They will possess diamond strength and a happiness equal to that of a monk absorbed in deep meditation and bliss. Whatever teaching they wish to hear, they will hear.

**About the Buddha he will become:** His light will be immeasurable, his life immeasurable, his assembly beyond counting, and Buddhas in every direction will sing his praises.

**About salvation — the vows that made history:** Any being who hears his name and thinks of him with serene trust, who longs to be born in his land and builds up even a modest stock of merit,   even one who thinks of him only ten times, will be born there. The only exceptions are those who have committed the five unforgivable sins or slandered the true teaching. And when such a person comes to die, he will appear before them in person, surrounded by monks, so that they die with undisturbed minds. Bodhisattvas in other worlds who hear his name will never fall away from enlightenment; they will gain deep concentration, be born into noble families, and their joy will never fail. Even women in other worlds who hear his name and turn toward enlightenment will never again be reborn in a female form they wish to leave behind. In his land, clothes appear fresh upon the body the moment they are thought of, and whatever offerings a bodhisattva wishes to make to other Buddhas appear instantly in their hands.

When Dharmakara finished, the earth shook, flowers rained from the sky, music sounded, and a voice declared: "You will become a Buddha."

## 4. The Bodhisattva's Long Career

For countless ages afterward, Dharmakara lived as a bodhisattva in perfect discipline, never conceiving a thought of lust, malice, or cruelty; gentle, content, honest, and tireless. Wherever he was born, treasures rose from the earth for him to give away. A fragrance like sandalwood issued from his mouth and lotus-perfume from his pores, and he led innumerable beings toward enlightenment.

## 5. Amitabha and His Land

That monk, the Buddha told Ananda, is now the Buddha Amitabha, Immeasurable Light, also called Amitayus, Immeasurable Life. He dwells in the western direction, beyond billions of worlds, in the land called *Sukhavati*. His light fills the universe; his lifespan cannot be measured; ten cosmic ages have passed since his enlightenment. His assembly is beyond counting: if millions of people with supernatural powers spent eons counting just the first circle of his disciples, they would not reach a fraction of it, like one drop of water lifted from the ocean on the tip of a split hair.

## 6. The Land of Bliss

Sukhavati is prosperous, fertile, and lovely. There are no hells, no animals, no ghosts, and no mountains either: the land is level everywhere, smooth as the palm of a hand, rich with treasures.

**The jewel trees.** The land is adorned with trees made of the seven precious substances, gold, silver, beryl, crystal, coral, red pearl, and diamond. Some trees are made of a single jewel; others of two, three, or all seven at once. Roots of gold, trunks of silver, branches of beryl, leaves of coral, flowers of pearl, fruit of diamond, in every combination. They are fragrant and pleasant to the touch, and when the wind moves through them they give off a sweet, enchanting sound that never wearies the ear. The land is hung with golden nets and covered with jewel lotuses, some a mile across, some many miles, and from every lotus pour billions of rays of light, and from every ray arise Buddhas who travel to all the worlds to teach.

**The rivers.** Great rivers flow there, miles wide and deep, carrying perfumed water and floating flowers, their banks lined with jewel trees. Their murmuring is like heavenly music, and it seems to say: *impermanent, peaceful, unreal.* When beings wade in, the water rises to the ankle, the knee, the shoulder, or the ear — exactly as each one wishes — and turns warm or cool as desired. The sound of the water teaches whatever each listener needs to hear: Buddha, Dharma, Sangha, the perfections, emptiness, compassion, equanimity. And whoever does not wish to hear it hears nothing. In that land there is no sound of sin, misfortune, or pain — there is not even a word for them.

**Every wish fulfilled.** The beings there live like the highest gods. They do not eat solid food — whatever meal they long for, they experience as if tasted, and are satisfied without eating. If they wish for perfumes, the whole land is scented; if they wish for music, banners, robes, or jewels, the whole land shines with them, and they find themselves clothed and adorned. If they wish for a palace of a certain height and design, it appears before them, and they dwell in it in delight.

**The flower rains.** Each morning the winds stir the jewel trees, and blossoms fall until the whole ground lies buried seven fathoms deep in soft, fragrant flowers — a bed of petals that sinks four inches underfoot and springs back when you lift your foot. By noon they vanish without a trace, the ground fresh and clean, and new flowers fall — and so it continues through every watch of the day.

**No sun, no night.** There is no mention of fire, sun, moon, or stars, no blinding darkness — not even the words "day" and "night," except as a figure of speech. The land is lit by the Buddha's own light. Clouds shower down perfumed water, flowers, and jeweled ornaments; music plays in the sky.

**The people.** Everyone born there is fixed in truth until final nirvana. They have no idea of "mine," no idea of self and other, no strife, no dispute. They are serene, impartial, tender, and wise — patient as the earth, cleansing as water, burning away pride like fire, unattached as wind, unstained by the world like lotuses rising from mud.

## 7. How Beings Get There

All the Buddhas in every direction praise Amitabha's name, and whoever hears it with even one moment of joyful trust will never fall away from enlightenment. Those who revere him deeply, build up merit, and turn their hearts toward his land will see Amitabha himself at the moment of death, surrounded by monks, and be reborn in Sukhavati. Those whose devotion and merit are weaker will see a vision of him — and be born there too. Even those who can manage only ten mindful thoughts of him, who rejoice in the teaching and long for that land, will see him in dreams and be born there.

## 8. The Doubters in the Lotus Buds

The Buddha then turned to Maitreya. Those who long for Sukhavati without any doubt are born there instantly, sitting cross-legged on open lotus flowers. But those who waver — who amass merit yet secretly doubt — are born inside closed lotus buds, where they must wait five hundred years. It is like a prince locked in a golden dungeon: the palace is jeweled, the couch soft, the food exquisite — yet he wants only to be free. The doubters live in comfort, but they cannot see the Buddha, hear the teaching, or grow in merit. "See what a loss doubt brings," the Buddha said. "Do not enter that jeweled prison."

## 9. The Vision

At Ananda's request, the Buddha told him to stand and face west. Instantly Amitabha sent out a ray of light from the palm of his hand, and the entire assembly — monks, laypeople, gods, and spirits — saw the Land of Bliss: Amitabha towering like Mount Sumeru, his bodhisattvas blazing with light, the jewel trees and rivers and flower-strewn ground. And the beings of Sukhavati, in that same moment, saw our world and the Buddha Shakyamuni teaching within it.

## 10. The Closing Charge

The Buddha entrusted the sutra to Maitreya: "Hear it, learn it, copy it, teach it — even to keep a copy for a single night bears immeasurable fruit. Buddhas rarely appear in the world; teachings like this are rarer still. Do not doubt. Do not let this teaching perish." As he finished, countless beings attained awakening, the universe trembled, music sounded through the heavens, and the whole assembly — human and divine — rejoiced.
