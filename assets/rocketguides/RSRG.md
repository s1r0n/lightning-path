## The Rocket Scientists' Guide to Religion

This draft argues that the scholarly failure to define religion stems not merely from religion's genuine complexity (which resists the Occam-driven sound-bite reductions of Marx, Freud, Durkheim, and James) but from two deeper pathologies: ontological bias hardened into identity through what Bacevic calls epistemic attachment, and a structural "Boundary Fetish" — the Eurocentric, frequently racist project, running from Tylor, Frazer, Comte, and Malinowski through the secularization thesis (recanted even by Berger) and Masuzawa's "invention of world religions" (pluralism as repackaged hierarchy, with Christianity "first among unequals"), of inscribing a hierarchy with Indigenous knowledge at the bottom, Western monotheism in the middle, and Western science at the top.

The draft names the agents of this project, after Marx, [Conceptive Ideologists](https://spiritwiki.lightningpath.org/iindex.ph/Conceptive_Ideologist) — producers of ideas who draw the boundaries their class needs drawn and package them as neutral scholarship — illustrating the mechanism via Sinclair Broadcasting's must-run scripts, Bezos's 2025 editorial seizure of the Washington Post, and the institutional murder of Humanistic Psychology (per Elkins), a tradition marginalized not because its claims failed but because it threatened materialist gatekeeping.

Surveying substantive definitions (Tylor, Spiro, Wallace, Comte, Feuerbach, Durkheim), the draft adopts Smart's seven dimensions plus an added eighth, the therapeutic/healing dimension (documented across Catholicism, Buddhism, Indigenous shamanism and the medicine bag complex, Ayurveda, and Islamic ruqyah), then tests these as discriminating criteria. Only one element does real boundary work: the Path — an aspirational, ethical, and legal trajectory aimed at something sacred. That criterion excludes sports and pop concerts but uncomfortably admits science (whose sacred aspiration is truth) as a religion, or at least as religion-like, a conclusion the draft accepts rather than evades.

Turning to functional definitions (Malinowski, Freud, Spiro, Yinger, Durkheim), the draft grounds religion in the Circle of Seven Essential Needs (physiological, emotional, cognitive, psychological, environmental, alignment, connection), defining religion as an institution essentially focused on answering existential imperatives and facilitating alignment and connection needs. It then acknowledges two residual problems. First, comprehensive substantive-functional definitions dissolve the boundary problem entirely: science, the Boy Scouts, and the New Age movement all check every substantive and functional box, and the ontological and epistemological last defenses fail under Kuhn's paradigms, the Sheldrake affair, and the documented mystical experiences of working scientists. Second, the definition is sociologically naive until supplemented with non-essential needs — religion's recurrent hijacking by elite interests to serve their class-based needs for for power, control, and profit.

That hijacking is evidenced by Ardashir and Tanser's canonization and violent standardization of Zoroastrianism, Constantine's co-optation of Christianity, the scribal insertion of patriarchal passages (1 Timothy 2; 1 Corinthians 14) documented by Ehrman, and elite fingerprints on the Tarot (A.E. Waite) and Theosophy (Blavatsky) — a pattern the draft notes sociologists themselves have largely missed.

The final definition — religion as a complicated, flexible institution meeting essential and non-essential human needs, organized around big-question answers with alignment and connection at its core — is offered not as a boundary solution but as a deliberate boundary dissolution. The overarching LP thesis: clear lines between Indigenous spirituality, Western religion, science, and other institutions simply do not exist, and scholarly progress requires abandoning cultural arrogance for respect, reciprocity, and open empirical observation in the Weberian spirit.
